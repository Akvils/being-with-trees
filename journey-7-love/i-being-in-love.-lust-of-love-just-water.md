## Love for the Land

Modern romance has taught us to seek in a partner what our ancestors found in the land.

We expect our human relationships to provide the sense of belonging, safety, and wholeness that once arose from living in intimate relationship with the natural world. We ask another person to become our home, our ground, our source of stability. Yet no human being, no matter how loving or conscious, can carry the weight of an entire world.

For hundreds of thousands of years, human life unfolded in continuous relationship with the land. Our bodies knew the soil beneath our feet, the changing seasons, the rhythms of water, the shelter of trees, and the presence of others with whom we shared a place. The land was not a backdrop to life. It was the ground of our existence, shaping our senses, our rhythms, and our understanding of belonging.

As we have become increasingly separated from the natural world, we have lost something of this relationship. Yet the need for belonging remains. We carry it into our friendships, our communities, and our romantic relationships, often expecting other people to satisfy a hunger they were never meant to satisfy alone.

We seek reassurance when we feel ungrounded. We fear abandonment when we have lost our sense of home. We ask others to regulate what we have forgotten how to hold within ourselves. The result is that love becomes entangled with need, and intimacy becomes burdened by the expectation that another person should make us feel whole.

Perhaps we have misunderstood what it means to love.

To love another person is not to make them our world. It is to share a world with them. And to do so, we must first recover our relationship with the world that exists beyond us.

The land offers a different kind of intimacy. It does not ask us to explain ourselves, perform an identity, or become someone more worthy of acceptance. We can sit beneath a tree with our grief, walk through a forest with our confusion, or remain beside a river without needing to arrive at an answer. The encounter asks only that we become present to what is here.

This presence is not an escape from human relationships. It changes how we enter them.

When we learn to sit with ourselves in the presence of the land, we may become less compelled to make another person responsible for our inner state. We can listen without immediately trying to fix, remain close without possessing, and allow another person the freedom to be different from us. We can meet their sorrow without being consumed by it and their joy without feeling diminished by it.

The land also gives us somewhere to take what we do not yet know how to carry. When we are angry, confused, or overwhelmed, we can walk, sit, breathe, and allow the experience to unfold before bringing it into our relationships. This does not mean that nature resolves every conflict or that we no longer need one another. It means that our relationships need not bear the full weight of our unexamined reactions.

When someone we love needs space, we may still feel the ache of separation. But we can learn to remain with that ache without immediately turning it into a story of abandonment. We can return to the woods, feel the ground beneath us, and remember that our belonging extends beyond any single relationship. We can love someone without making their constant presence the condition of our own stability.

There is a quiet generosity in this. When we establish a relationship with the land, we release other people from the impossible task of being everything to us. We allow love to become less about filling an emptiness and more about meeting another being as they are.

This is not a reason to withdraw from human intimacy. The land cannot replace the tenderness of a human embrace, the vulnerability of being known, or the shared life that grows between two people. Our relationships with one another remain essential. But they can unfold differently when they are rooted in a belonging that is not dependent on another person's attention or approval.

Perhaps our ancestors did not experience the separation between human love and love for the land in the way many of us do today. The place where they lived, the beings around them, and the people with whom they shared their lives belonged to one continuous field of relationship. We may not be able to return to their way of living, but we can begin to recover something of that intimacy.

To love the land is to become attentive to the place that sustains us. It is to notice the life around us, to develop familiarity with the rhythms of a place, and to allow our relationship with it to deepen through time. It is to move beyond seeing nature as a resource for our wellbeing and begin to encounter it as a living world of which we are a part.

When this love deepens, the land is no longer somewhere we go to feel better. It becomes a relationship in its own right, one that asks for our attention, care, and participation.

And perhaps this is where love begins to change. We stop approaching the world only in terms of what it can give us. We begin to care for what exists beyond our own needs. The same movement can enter our relationships with people: a shift from possessing to appreciating, from demanding to participating, from needing another to complete our world to discovering what we can share within it.

To love another human being well, we must remember that neither they nor we exist in isolation. We belong to a world that precedes us and will continue beyond us. The more deeply we experience this belonging, the more freely we may be able to love.

To love another well, we must first remember the world to which we belong.
